\section{Computational notes}
\label{sec:computation}

This section collects the sizes of the reformulations and what was measured on two benchmarks:
the $T$-period inventory problem of \file{benchmark/inventory/timing.jl} (one scalar uniform demand per stage, a box support, $T+1$ stages),
and the hydrothermal problem of \citet{ShapiroEtAl2013} in \file{benchmark/hydro} (4 reservoirs, one joint discrete law of 4 inflows per stage, independent across stages).
All times are from HiGHS \citep{HuangfuHall2018} with default options unless stated, on one machine (Windows 11, Julia 1.12.6);
the result files and the analyses are in \file{benchmark/inventory} and \file{benchmark/hydro}, in a folder \file{results} and a \file{README.md} each;
the comparison of solve times with the previous version is summarized in the section ``Past studies'' of \file{benchmark/README.md}.
The later studies of \file{benchmark/breakpoints}, \file{benchmark/scaling} and \file{benchmark/solvers} add two problems of \file{test/problems}:
the inventory of \citet[Section~5]{KWG2011} and the production planning of \citet[Section~6.1]{GKW2019}.

\subsection{Where the sizes come from}

Each part of the reformulation is attached to one row of the model and grows with what that row sees.
For an inequality $r$ that is not deterministic, with touched support blocks $\Touch_r$ (\cref{sec:primal}):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
& variables & constraints \\
\midrule
rules & $\sum_i \nvis_i$ in total & \\
equality row $r$ & & $\abs{\Jr_r}$ \\
primal, inequality $r$ & $\sum_{b\in\Touch_r} q_b$ & $1 + \sum_{b\in\Touch_r} k_b$ \\
dual, inequality $r$ & $1 + \abs{\Jr_r}$ & $1 + \abs{\Jr_r} + (\text{rows of } \Wp \text{ with a slack term})$ \\
dense primal \citep[(4.2)]{KWG2011} & $2 + \sum_b q_b$ & $1 + k$ \\
dense dual \citep[(4.6)]{KWG2011} & & $1 + \sum_b q_b$, each with up to $k$ slack terms \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
In the dense dual each slack term $S_{rj}$ is itself an expression of $X$, and the matrix $\Mom\Wp^\T$ is dense because of the rank-one term $\mub\mub^\T$;
its nonzeros grow as the number of inequalities times the number of rows of $W$ times $k$.
In the structured dual the rank-one term is carried by the single variable $u_r$,
and a row of $\Wp$ gets a slack term only from the structural columns that share a covariance block with its support block.

In a multistage problem the history grows with the stage, so even the structured formulations grow quadratically with $T$ when the stage dimension is fixed:
a stage-$t$ decision has $1 + \sum_{s \le t}(\text{dimension of stage } s)$ coefficients.
For the inventory problem the counts are exact polynomials (\cref{app:sizes}):
the structured primal has $\tfrac{11}{2}T^2 + \tfrac72 T$ variables with multipliers on the boxes ($\tfrac72 T^2 + \tfrac72 T$ with the closed form, the default) and $\tfrac52 T^2 + \tfrac{11}{2}T - 2$ constraints,
the structured dual $\tfrac72T^2 + \tfrac{23}{2}T - 4$ variables and $\tfrac{13}{2}T^2 + \tfrac{19}{2}T - 4$ constraints,
all matching the measured models;
the nonzeros of the dense dual of the previous version grow as $T^3$ (279 thousand, 2.17 million and 17.0 million at $T = 32$, 64 and 128),
those of the structured dual as $T^2$ (17.6, 69.0 and 273 thousand).

\subsection{Smaller is not always faster}
\label{sec:faster}

Solve times do not follow the sizes, because presolve does much of the same work (\file{benchmark/README.md}, ``Past studies'').
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Primal.} After presolve, the 0.4 primal with multipliers on the boxes and the legacy primal with \code{SlackAsExpr} are the same linear program
(at hydrothermal $T = 12$, both about 42.2 thousand rows, 150.5 thousand columns and 390.9 thousand nonzeros),
and they take the same time (161 s and 168 s); differences of $\pm30\%$ in iterations come from the order of rows and columns alone.
The smaller formulation saves the time to build and to read the model, and wins clearly without presolve (1.4 s against 6.8 s at inventory $T = 128$).
\item \emph{Dual.} The structured dual is 6 to 150 times faster than the dense one on the inventory problem, from $T = 32$ to $T = 128$ (6.9 s against 1048 s at $T = 128$),
but 4 to 6.5 times slower on the hydrothermal problem (145 s against 22 s at $T = 8$).
There, presolve reduces the dense dual much more (from 200,648 to 33,455 rows, merging pairs of rows into ranges) than the structured one (from 103,280 to 66,743 rows),
and the dual simplex needs four times more iterations.
The cause is the auxiliary $u_r = S_r\mub$: substituting it out gives back the presolved model and the iteration counts of the dense dual on the hydrothermal problem (39 s instead of 145 s at $T = 8$),
but makes the inventory dual 20 times slower (15.2 s instead of 0.72 s at $T = 64$), where the denser rows cost more than the rows saved.
Neither form is better in general, and the package keeps $u_r$.
A density rule choosing per row whether to keep it is not implemented:
the two problems above point in opposite directions, and a threshold on the number of columns of a row would need more instances to be chosen
(\file{benchmark/README.md}, ``Past studies'').
\item \emph{Redundant rows of a lifting.} The support of an uncertainty with $n$ breakpoints needs $n+2$ rows, and the box of its lifted coordinates adds $2n$ implied ones (\cref{sec:axis-lifting}).
Leaving them out saves 30\% to 43\% of the primal variables and 26\% to 37\% of the dual rows, with the same values.
The primal was never measurably slower for it (106 s instead of 187 s on the hydrothermal problem with one breakpoint per inflow at $T = 8$),
and the dual was 8\% to 47\% faster on the inventory problem and on the hydrothermal one at $T = 4$, but 2.6 times slower at $T = 8$ (276 s instead of 105 s), under every seed of HiGHS tried.
So \code{PrimalLDR} leaves them out and \code{DualLDR} keeps them, by default (\code{lifted\_box}; \file{benchmark/README.md}, ``Past studies'').
A wider study, on four problems and four modes of HiGHS, before the rescaling of \cref{sec:rescaling} (\file{benchmark/breakpoints/README.md}), confirmed the choice of the primal, except for the production planning with 3 breakpoints;
for the dual, leaving them out was faster on 13 of the 15 instances solved both ways by the interior point with crossover, by up to 20 times,
but slower by 1.4 to 2.4 times on three of the six hydrothermal instances under the dual simplex.
The default of the dual is kept until the study is repeated with the rescaling.
\item \emph{Solver mode for the bound.} The dual approximation is solved for its value only, and its rule is not a policy, so no basis is needed.
HiGHS's interior-point method without crossover solved the hydrothermal dual at $T = 8$ in 8.0 s instead of 145 s,
with an objective equal to the simplex one to a relative $2\cdot10^{-10}$, but was slower on the inventory dual (1.2 s against 0.72 s).
The primal and the dual approximations take raw attributes of their inner solver, each for itself only,
such as \code{DualLDR(; attributes = ["solver" => "ipm", "run\_crossover" => "off"])};
none is the default, since the names are those of one solver and the best mode depends on the problem.
A bound taken from an inexact solve is safest when it is the inner solver's dual objective value, which bounds the inner optimum by weak duality;
the package uses it for a linear program solved to optimality and for a solve stopped by a limit with a dual feasible point (\cref{sec:dual}).
Only an \code{OPTIMAL} status can be trusted, though: HiGHS may stop an interior point without crossover at an unknown status,
with a wrong objective value or none, and the package then reports no bound.
On the wider grid of \file{benchmark/breakpoints}, this happened on every dual of the inventory of \citet{KWG2011}, and on most primals and duals of the production planning;
the interior point with crossover was the fastest mode on the largest duals.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Breakpoints and solve time}

Breakpoints multiply the size by about the number of pieces per uncertainty.
On the hydrothermal problem, with $k$ breakpoints per inflow at the equiprobable quantiles of its marginal law (\cref{tab:breakpoints}),
one breakpoint halves the primal value at $T = 4$ and divides it by 2.5 at $T = 8$,
while the dual bound moves by at most 1\%;
the primal problem has 2.6 times more variables and takes 5.2 times (at $T = 4$) to 5.8 times (at $T = 8$) longer to solve.
The out-of-sample means of the primal policies, simulated on 2000 outcomes of the joint law, agree with the primal values within 1.3 standard errors, and no constraint is violated.
The breakpoint variants declare the inflows of a stage as independent marginals (breakpoints apply to univariate declarations);
with no breakpoint this changes nothing in the primal value, the cost being linear, but it splits the covariance blocks, and the dual has 42\% fewer nonzeros (57,296 against 99,344 at $T = 4$).

\subsection{Rescaling the uncertainties}
\label{sec:rescaling}

The approximations do not depend on the units of the uncertainties, but the inner problems do.
A demand uniform on $[8000, 12000]$ has a second moment of order $10^8$, against $1$ for the constant column of $\xi$,
and the robust counterparts and the cone rows of the dual mix coefficients of both orders;
on such data the inner solvers can stop at an unknown status, or take much longer (below).
Before the formulations, \code{optimize!} therefore writes each uncertainty $\eta_j$ as $\eta_j = c_j + s_j\zeta_j$, that is
\[
\xi = G\hat\xi,
\qquad
\hat\xi = (1, \zeta),
\qquad
G = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c & D_s \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D_s = \operatorname{diag}(s),
\]
where $c_j$ is the center and $s_j$ the half-width of the box of the law of $\eta_j$, so that $\zeta_j \in [-1, 1]$;
for a law whose box is not bounded (or is a point), they are its mean and standard deviation if this is positive and finite,
and otherwise $c_j = 0$ and $s_j = 1$
(\code{RescaleUncertainties}, true by default; \file{src/formulations/rescaling.jl}).
The data of \eqref{eq:SP} in $\hat\xi$ are
\[
\hat B = BG, \quad
\hat C = CG, \quad
\hat Q = D_sQD_s, \quad
\hat d = D_s(d + 2Qc), \quad
\hat f = f + d^\T c + c^\T Q c,
\]
for each of $B_e$, $B_u$ and $B_l$;
the rows $W\eta - h \in \mathcal{K}$ of $\Xis$ become $WD_s\zeta - (h - Wc) \in \mathcal{K}$;
the law of $\zeta$ is the image of that of $\eta$, with mean $D_s^{-1}(\mu - c)$ and covariance blocks $D_s^{-1}\Cvb{\ell}D_s^{-1}$ (restricted to the coordinates of the block), so that $\hat\Mom = G^{-1}\Mom G^{-\T}$;
and the constants of the parameters shift like the first column of $BG$.
Since $D_s$ is diagonal, the covariance blocks, the support blocks, the boxes and the information structure are those of $\xi$,
and the inner problems have the same sizes.
The formulations solve the problem in $\hat\xi$, and the rules are reported in $\xi$, as $X = \hat XG^{-1}$.

\begin{proposition}[Invariance under an affine change of the uncertainties]
\label{prop:affine-invariance}
Let $G = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ c & D_s \end{psmallmatrix}$ with $s_j \ne 0$ for every $j$,
and let $\widehat{\textup{SP}}$ be the problem in $\hat\xi = G^{-1}\xi$ with the data above.
Then $X \mapsto XG$ is a bijection of the rules allowed by the information structure, $\Rules$,
which maps the feasible points of \eqref{eq:PLDR}, \eqref{eq:DLDR} and \eqref{eq:SLDR} (with the outcomes $G^{-1}\xi^{(n)}$) to the feasible points of the same problems for $\widehat{\textup{SP}}$, with the same objective values.
Condition~\ref{it:lce} of \cref{thm:dual-bound} holds for one problem if and only if it holds for the other.
In particular, the values, the bounds and the rules $X = \hat XG^{-1}$ do not depend on $c$ and $s$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first row of $G$ and of $G^{-1} = \begin{psmallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -D_s^{-1}c & D_s^{-1} \end{psmallmatrix}$ is $\eone^\T$, so $\hat\xi_1 = \xi_1 = 1$.
Column $j > 1$ of $XG$ is $s_j$ times column $j$ of $X$, and its first column is $X_{:,1} + \sum_{j > 1} c_jX_{:,j}$.
So $XG$ has the zero pattern of $X$ outside the first column, which every rule observes, and the same holds for $G^{-1}$:
$X \mapsto XG$ is a bijection of $\Rules$.
For every outcome, $x(\xi) = X\xi = XG\hat\xi$ and $B\xi = BG\hat\xi$,
so the decisions, their bounds, the right-hand sides and the objective are the same,
and the coefficient matching of the equalities, $A_eX = B_e$, holds if and only if $A_eXG = B_eG$.
The support is mapped to $G^{-1}\Xis = \{\hat\xi : \hat\xi_1 = 1,\ WD_s\zeta - (h - Wc) \in \mathcal{K}\}$,
so an inequality holds on $\Xis$ if and only if it holds on $G^{-1}\Xis$,
which gives the claim for \eqref{eq:PLDR}, and for \eqref{eq:SLDR} on the mapped outcomes.
For \eqref{eq:DLDR}, the rows of $\Xis$ as a cone become $\Wp G$, and the slack of inequality $r$ becomes $\hat S_r = S_rG$, so that
\[
\hat S_r \hat\Mom (\Wp G)^\T = S_r G G^{-1}\Mom G^{-\T} G^\T \Wp^\T = S_r\Mom\Wp^\T:
\]
the cone constraints are unchanged, and the slack of a deterministic inequality, which has only a first column, is unchanged too ($S_rG = S_r$).
Finally, each $\zeta_j$ is an affine function of $\eta_j$ alone, in the same stage,
so the conditional expectations of the later columns given the earlier ones are affine in one set of coordinates if and only if they are in the other.
\end{proof}

The proposition also justifies \cref{rem:shifted-lifting} ($s = \one$ there).
It holds for any description of $\Xis$; two more steps keep the numbers of the rescaled rows of the order of 1.
First, each rescaled linear row is divided by its largest coefficient, and the rows of a second-order cone by one common factor, the largest of their coefficients:
a positive factor leaves a row of the orthant and a whole second-order cone unchanged (a factor per row would change a second-order cone), so neither approximation changes (\cref{prop:description}).
Second, a right-hand side $h_p - W_pc$ within $16\varepsilon(\abs{h_p} + \abs{W_p}\abs{c})$ of zero, the rounding error of its subtraction, is set to zero.
Without them, the first version of the rescaling kept the hull rows of the lifted coordinates, scaled by products $\Delta_i\Delta_{i+1}$ of the lengths of the pieces, with coefficients of order $10^6$ for demands of order $10^4$, and a first right-hand side made of rounding errors;
HiGHS's simplex then failed or reached the limit on 5 of the 6 primals of the production planning below, which it solves in units of the half-width.

\paragraph{With breakpoints.}
The rescaling applies to the lifted form, so each lifted coordinate is rescaled on its own box (\cref{sec:axis-lifting}):
$[\underline\eta, b_1]$ for the first piece and $[0, \Delta_i]$ for the others.
Then $\zeta_i = 2t_i - 1$ in the notation of \cref{lem:hull}, whose simplex becomes
\[
1 \ge \zeta_1 \ge \zeta_2 \ge \dots \ge \zeta_{n+1} \ge -1,
\]
with the hull rows $\zeta_i - \zeta_{i+1} \ge 0$ once divided by their largest coefficient $\Delta_i\Delta_{i+1}/2$, all with a zero right-hand side.
The uncertainty is
\[
\eta = \frac{\underline\eta + \overline\eta}{2} + \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} \frac{\Delta_i}{2}\zeta_i,
\quad\text{that is}\quad
\eta = \frac{\underline\eta + \overline\eta}{2} + \frac{\overline\eta - \underline\eta}{2(n+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1} \zeta_i
\quad\text{for $n$ equally spaced breakpoints.}
\]
A piece is then $-1$, not $0$, below its breakpoint, and the constant column of $\hat B$ collects the centers of all the pieces.
The alternative is to rescale the uncertainty before lifting it,
\[
\eta = \frac{\underline\eta + \overline\eta}{2} + \frac{\overline\eta - \underline\eta}{2}\zeta,
\]
and to lift $\zeta$ by \eqref{eq:axis-lifting}:
its pieces are $0$ below their breakpoint, but the first one lies in $[-1, -1 + 2\Delta_1/(\overline\eta - \underline\eta)]$,
a short interval far from zero, nearly a multiple of the constant column when the pieces are many.
The two differ by a map $G$, so they have the same values;
which one makes the inner problems easier is open (below).

\paragraph{Measured.}
The production planning of \citet[Section~6.1]{GKW2019} (\file{test/problems/production.jl}) has five demands of order $10^4$ per week, independent and uniform, here with $k = 1$ or $3$ equally spaced breakpoints each.
The same problem was written with the demands as uncertainties (``raw''), with their deviations from the nominal demands, and with these deviations divided by their half-width, in $[-1, 1]$ (``unit'', which rescales before lifting),
all three without the rescaling of the package;
and with the raw demands and the rescaling of the package (``rescaled'').
\Cref{tab:scaling} gives the times with HiGHS's dual simplex.
The values agree to $2\cdot10^{-8}$ relative.
Without rescaling, HiGHS solves none of the six duals with the raw demands, one with the deviations and four in the unit form;
with the rescaling of the package, it solves five, in about the times of the unit form.
Centering alone is not enough: it solves one more dual than the raw demands, but reaches the limit on the primal with $T = 20$ and $k = 1$, which takes 24~s with the raw demands.
The primal approximation changes less: apart from that instance, HiGHS reaches the limit only on the largest one ($T = 20$, $k = 3$), with the raw demands and the deviations;
but it was 1.2 to 4.5 times slower with the rescaling of the package than in the unit form on the six primals (measured on different commits).
On the inventory of \citet[Section~5]{KWG2011}, with demands of order $10^3$, the rescaling made faster the three duals that HiGHS solved (at $T = 48$, $k = 1$: 141~s against 357~s),
and the values agree to $5\cdot10^{-10}$.
The details are in \file{benchmark/scaling/README.md}.

\subsection{Building the inner problems}

The reformulations are built from sparse blocks, row by row, as JuMP models;
objectives are assembled term by term with \code{dot}, never as traces of matrix products.
The variables of the inner problems are unnamed by default, since names slow the build down; \code{names = true} gives names such as \code{X[i,j]}, \code{$\lambda$[r,b]}, \code{u[r]}, \code{S[r,j]} for inspection.
The dense formulations of the previous version are kept in \file{test/oracles/dense.jl}, and the tests require that both agree.
